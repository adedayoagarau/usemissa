## What makes a literary magazine “top tier”?

Usually a mix of prize recognition, readership, pay and editorial history, and each of those measures something different.

Prize anthologies show which published pieces other editors picked. Contributor pay tells you about the terms writers get. Readership is about who might come across your work. None of them makes one magazine the best home for every manuscript.

So pick the measure first. If you want readers of short literary fiction, a magazine built around that form may matter more than a bigger name. If you’ve written a short personal essay, find a journal that publishes those. Don’t push it through a fiction category because the title looks impressive.

Tiers help when they make those choices visible. They mislead when they squash everything into one label. A journal can pay well and reply slowly, publish excellent work to a small readership, or be exactly right for one community.

## How do literary magazine rankings work?

Each ranking counts something different, so read its method before you use its order to choose where to submit.

[Clifford Garstang’s 2026 overview](https://cliffordgarstang.com/2026-literary-magazine-rankings-overview/) ranks magazines by Pushcart Prizes and Special Mentions over a rolling ten years, with recent recognition counting more, and keeps fiction, nonfiction and poetry separate. It doesn’t count submission fees, contributor pay or response time, and it leaves out commercial magazines such as The New Yorker and Harper’s.

That makes it a good measure of one kind of recognition. It doesn’t make the top journal the cheapest, the easiest to reach or the right place for your story.

[Missa’s ranking method](/rankings/methodology) scores each magazine out of 100 across six measures: honors, pay, reply time, fees, submission policy, and archive and ethics. Honors, mainly Pushcart recognition, carry the most weight, at up to 40 points. A fact nobody has recorded earns half its points, so part of a score can reflect what’s missing. Look at the record behind any score. This describes the published method; it isn’t an outside audit of the data.

A low reported rate is no substitute for reading the magazine, and it doesn’t earn a place in a reputation ranking. ## What are the best magazines for poetry, fiction and essays?

The best ones for you publish the form and length you’ve written, so match those first and weigh reputation after.

### Poetry

Start with magazines whose recent poems show interest in the forms you write. Poetry, The Paris Review, Ploughshares, The Kenyon Review and AGNI each read differently, and their names aren’t interchangeable. Read a run of poems from a recent issue, so one poem doesn’t become your whole picture of the magazine.

Notice range, length, how the poems use the page and how a group of poems hangs together. Send a coherent set of your strongest eligible poems. Don’t pad a packet with weaker work to reach the maximum.

The [Poetry submission page](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poetrymagazine/submit) says submitting is free and lists current pay rates. The [Paris Review guidelines](https://www.theparisreview.org/about/submissions) set separate online months for poetry and take postal submissions all year. Details like these shape your schedule as much as reputation does.

For a wider search, browse [poetry calls](/discover/poetry) and check whether each result is a magazine, contest, residency or grant. Each asks you for a different decision, even under the same poetry label.

### Fiction

Match the story’s length and form before you rank anything by reputation. [One Story](https://one-story.com/write/submit-a-story/) asks for literary stories of 3,000 to 8,000 words, so a flash piece isn’t a fit for its general submissions, however good it is.

For broader literary fiction, read The Paris Review, Ploughshares, AGNI, The Kenyon Review and A Public Space, then find the real matches. Check whether they take excerpts, translations or work with genre elements, and go by the magazine’s own wording, not a directory label.

Routes change. The New Yorker’s [contact page](https://www.newyorker.com/about/contact) says its fiction email route closed in August 2026 and that fiction reopens through Submittable on November 1, 2026. An older article telling you to email your story is out of date. Check each route right before you send, even when the manuscript’s ready.

Search [fiction calls](/discover/fiction) for what’s open now, and keep a note of places to revisit when their next window opens.

### Essays

Know what kind of essay you have before you choose: personal, flash, criticism, a dispatch or reported work.

Brevity takes essays of 750 words or fewer. The Sun publishes personal essays alongside other forms. The Common cares about place and has separate guidance for dispatches. A Public Space welcomes essays and work that doesn’t fit a genre label.

Read the right section. A review, interview or editorial essay in an issue doesn’t mean the magazine takes unsolicited work in that form. Some sections are commissioned or need a pitch.

Check whether the piece has been online before. Some guidelines count a post on a personal website or social account as already published, whether or not you were paid for it.

Use [creative nonfiction calls](/discover/creative-nonfiction) to widen the search, then narrow by form and length. One clear match beats a long list of magazines that publish “nonfiction” in some form.

## Which literary magazines pay writers?

Several well-known magazines publish their rates: [Poetry](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poetrymagazine/submit) lists US$500 per text poem from its March 2026 issue, [One Story](https://one-story.com/write/submit-a-story/) lists US$500 per accepted story, and [Brevity](https://brevitymag.com/submissions/) lists a US$45 honorarium for featured essays.

Those are published examples, not rates for every category, and an individual contract can differ.

Paying and charging are separate questions. A paying magazine may still charge a submission fee, and a free one may still pay. Read our [guide to submission fees and tip jars](/guides/literary-magazine-submission-fees) before you spend money.

Before you accept, read the contract: rights, exclusivity, when you’re paid and contributor copies. A high ranking doesn’t settle whether those terms work for you. If something’s unclear, ask the editor before you agree.

## How should you order submissions across tiers?

By fit, the practical rules and your goals, with reputation as one factor among them.

If several good matches allow simultaneous submissions, send to a first group at once instead of waiting on each in turn. Check each policy, log every destination and withdraw quickly if the piece is taken elsewhere.

If a magazine wants exclusive consideration, decide whether the wait is worth it. You might keep one piece for that magazine and send other work where simultaneous submissions are fine. Don’t break an exclusivity rule to speed things up.

A good shortlist has some ambitious picks, some long-standing journals that fit well and some smaller magazines you value. The groups don’t need to be the same size. Keep the list small enough that you can track every reading period and withdrawal rule.

Look again as decisions arrive. A rejection on its own isn’t an instruction to revise, and a personal note isn’t a contract. Revise when you have a clear editorial reason, and keep track of which version each magazine has.

## Are newer online magazines worth submitting to?

Yes, when the work they publish and their terms meet your standards.

A newer magazine may have little prize history and too few tracker reports for useful statistics. Read what it has published, find out who the editors are and check that its submission terms are clear. A short record is a reason to look closely, not to rule it out.

Look at how it presents contributors, whether back issues stay online and how it explains rights and fees. If you want to reach a particular community or try a form, a smaller online magazine can be a strong choice.

Print isn’t a stand-in for quality. A stable online archive, careful editing and readers who care about your work can matter more than a printed contributor copy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there an official literary magazine tier list?

No. Each ranking follows its own method or one editor’s judgment, so read the criteria before you use it.

### Should I always submit to the most famous magazine first?

Only if the work fits and you’re fine with its rules, costs and wait. Reputation doesn’t make up for the wrong genre or length.

### Can a newer magazine be the best place for my work?

Yes. Read what it publishes and check its editing and terms. A strong match can matter more than a long prize record.

### How does Missa rank literary magazines?

Out of 100, across six measures. Honors, mainly Pushcart recognition, are worth up to 40 points. A missing fact earns half its points, and scores fall into four tiers. The [method page](/rankings/methodology) explains each measure.
