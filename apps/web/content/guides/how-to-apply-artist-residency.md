## What does a residency application ask for?

Most residency applications ask for two things: proof of your work, and a clear account of how you'd use the time, space or support on offer.

The details belong to each program. A writer might send a manuscript excerpt, a visual artist an image sequence, a composer recordings and scores. Some programs want a detailed proposal; others care more about finished work.

Build one folder before you settle your shortlist: a current CV, a short bio, a longer statement, your strongest samples, captions and technical descriptions. Keep a clean master of each document and export copies for each application, so cutting a statement for one program never touches the original.

Then make a requirements sheet for each residency: file types, word and image counts, excerpt length, naming rules, and whether the panel sees your name. Good work can still fail on a wrong file or a private video link. Name each file the way the program asks. If it doesn't say, `Surname_Sample1.pdf` beats `final_FINAL_v3.pdf`.

Before you send anything, run through this:

- [ ] Check who can apply: career stage, degree status, where you live, past residents
- [ ] Find the application fee, any waiver, and what attending would cost you
- [ ] Note the deadline, its time zone and any cap on applications
- [ ] Read the file rules: types, counts, word limits, running times, file names
- [ ] Pick samples that support the proposal
- [ ] Write the proposal for this program's time, space and resources
- [ ] Ask for references only if the program wants them
- [ ] Open every link on another device, logged out
- [ ] Submit, then save the receipt

## What do panels look for in a work sample?

Panels judge against each program's own criteria, and at MacDowell and Yaddo the work itself comes first. So a strong sample is your best eligible work that also backs up what you say you'll do there.

MacDowell says the [sole criterion](https://www.macdowell.org/apply/apply-for-fellowship) for acceptance is artistic excellence. Yaddo's [application page](https://yaddo.org/apply/) describes peer review and puts the weight on the quality of the work. Djerassi's [guidelines](https://djerassi.org/apply/application-guidelines/) describe panels of arts professionals in each category.

A famous referee won't rescue weak samples. And don't rule yourself out as too early in your career: check each program's career-stage, degree, past-resident and age rules. "Emerging" means different things in different places.

Then check that your samples hang together. A proposal about sound installations needs samples that show your decisions about sound, space or audience, not a spread picked to show range.

**Visual work.** Check exposure, scale and captions. Add installation views when the setting matters, and close details when they show something the full image can't. Give materials, dimensions and your part in any collaboration. Don't fill every slot with weaker pieces.

**Writing.** Pick an excerpt that stands on its own. If context is allowed, keep it short and factual, and get to the work fast. A long note about why the excerpt gets interesting later uses up time the sample needs.

**Film, performance and music.** If links are allowed, send one that opens without a login, and test the password and playback on another device. Point to the excerpt that matters and say what your role was. Stay inside the time limit. Sending the full recording ignores the format the panel asked for.

## How do I write a residency proposal?

A residency proposal says what you'll make or investigate, why this program suits it, and what you can realistically get done during the stay.

Answer four questions: what's the work, what stage is it at, what will you do there, and which of the program's resources matter? Keep the artistic question apart from the practical plan. You can leave the final outcome open and still be clear about the work you'll do.

Here's an invented outline that gives a panel something concrete:

*I'm developing a sequence of short essays about informal repair economies. During the residency I'll revise the finished opening essays and draft the next section, using interviews already recorded with participants' consent. The uninterrupted writing time and the program's library would support this stage. I expect to leave with a revised sequence and a plan for the remaining chapters.*

It's a structure to learn from, not text to copy. Write your own, and follow the program's rules on writing help. Add what's true for your project: permissions, equipment, and the limits of what you can finish.

Don't promise access you don't have. If the plan depends on an archive, a collaborator or a workshop, say whether that's confirmed, and give a fallback if it isn't. For a short stay, one focused phase of a longer project often reads clearer than a plan to finish everything.

## Do I need references, and when do I ask?

Only if the program asks for them. Check first whether it wants a letter or just contact details.

Yaddo currently says references aren't required. MacDowell has [temporarily suspended](https://www.macdowell.org/apply/application-guidelines) its reference letter requirement, and Skowhegan doesn't require letters of recommendation. Other programs may want a letter with the application or later in selection. Read the current instructions before you hand someone a deadline they don't need.

When you do need one, ask someone who knows the relevant work and can talk about it specifically. Ask if they're free before you type their email into a system that will contact them. Then send the program link, your project, your current materials, what the program wants them to cover, and the deadline with its time zone.

Give them time, and send one reminder if you need to. If the portal shows whether a letter arrived, check it. Sending the request doesn't mean the letter is in.

## How many residencies should I apply to at once?

Apply to as many good-fit programs as you can prepare properly with the time and money you have.

There's no proven right number. A handful of well-chosen programs can share core materials, but each still needs its own proposal, sample selection and eligibility check.

Here's a working schedule for one deadline. It's our advice, not any organizer's rule:

1. **Four weeks before:** check eligibility, funding and whether you could actually go. You want a shortlist you'd accept if selected.
2. **Three weeks before:** choose samples and ask for any required references. Finish with a complete materials folder.
3. **Two weeks before:** draft the proposal and fit it to this program.
4. **One week before:** check files, links, budget and requirements against the rules.
5. **A few days before:** submit and save the receipt.

Start earlier if passports, permissions or collaborators are involved, and leave room to fix an upload problem without counting on an extension. Save each residency call on Missa and you'll get reminders two weeks, one week, three days and one day before the deadline, in your time zone.

## What does a residency cost, and which ones pay you?

Count three costs separately: applying, attending, and what you'll spend yourself. Then set any stipend or grant against them.

Housing and a studio don't cover everything. Ask about meals, travel, materials, insurance, local transport and the rent you'll keep paying at home, and whether support arrives before you travel or after you've paid. The programs in the table show the range:

- MacDowell charges no residency fee and offers need-based stipends of [up to US$1,500](https://www.macdowell.org/apply/financial-assistance), plus travel reimbursement grants.
- Skowhegan charges US$6,000 tuition, but it's need-blind and says no accepted artist is turned away for lack of funding.
- The Albee Foundation charges nothing to apply or stay. Domestic fellows get a US$2,500 stipend and international fellows can claim expenses up to US$2,500, both paid after the residency.
- Jan van Eyck pays €1,500 a month, takes the rent out of it, and charges a €2,750 enrollment fee.
- PLOP's free studio space doesn't come with housing.

Those differences decide whether you can say yes to an offer, even when the application itself is cheap.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I apply without a long exhibition or publication history?

Yes, at some programs. Check each one's actual career-stage rules and send strong eligible samples, instead of ruling yourself out because of a program's reputation.

### Can I reuse a proposal?

Partly. Reuse your project's facts and core materials, then explain why this residency suits the work. Check every word limit and required document again.

### Are all residencies free to attend?

No. Some charge tuition or participation costs, and others cover set costs or offer financial aid. Free to apply and funded to attend are separate questions.

### Do I apply through Missa?

No. Each call on Missa links to the organizer's page, and you apply there.
