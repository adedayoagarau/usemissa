## How can I tell if an open call is a scam?

A scam lies about an offer, or about who’s behind it, to get your money, your information or something else. A fee on its own doesn’t make a call a scam.

Look for facts you can check yourself. Does the venue confirm the show? Can you find out who’s organizing it and who’s choosing the work? Are the application and post-selection costs both disclosed? Do the promised services come with dates and specifics?

Some offers are plain impersonation. [White Cube’s security guidance](https://www.whitecube.com/cyber-security-guidance) says it doesn’t charge fees for exhibitions or for reviewing work, and tells artists to check unexpected approaches through its own contact page. A paid invitation in White Cube’s name contradicts that published policy.

The [Royal Academy’s Summer Exhibition site](https://summer.royalacademy.org.uk/Home/) also warns that scammers are sending artists personal invitations to submit. A familiar logo and a real exhibition name don’t prove the sender represents the institution.

## What’s a vanity gallery?

A vanity gallery sells exhibition places to artists, often presenting the payment as a mark of selection or prestige.

The term describes a business model and a worry about value. It doesn’t prove that every service on offer is fraudulent. The useful questions are what you’re buying, whether selection means anything and whether the claims match the actual service.

A rented venue, an artist cooperative or a clearly priced group show can also charge artists. Judge those by their terms and purpose. A cooperative’s membership is a different thing from an unsolicited message implying a commercial gallery wants to represent you, followed by a request for money.

Ask whether the organizer earns mostly from selling places to artists, and how it shows that artists get what they’re promised. A photo of a busy opening doesn’t prove sales or lasting interest. Treat claims about collectors, curators and career boosts as questions that need evidence.

Our [guide to entry, hanging and participation fees](/guides/entry-fees-hanging-fees-participation-fees) explains the cost labels before you weigh up an offer.

## Is an unsolicited invitation to exhibit a red flag?

Not always, but it’s a reason to check carefully, especially when it asks for money, personal details or high-resolution files.

Look at the sender’s address and the reply-to address, then find the organizer’s official website yourself. A small spelling change in a domain is enough to impersonate someone. Real staff names get copied too, so finding that person on the website doesn’t prove the email came from them.

Then contact the organization through its own site and ask whether the invitation is real. Include the sender’s address and the key details, without unnecessary attachments. Don’t call a number that only appears in the suspicious message.

Specific claims should be checkable. An organizer who says they saw your work should be able to name it and say why it suits the show. Generic praise doesn’t prove dishonesty, but it gives you little reason to spend money.

> [!WARNING]
> Pressure to pay fast is the clearest red flag: a short payment deadline, a warning that you’ll lose your place, or a request to keep the invitation to yourself. Don’t pay until you’ve checked with the organization through its own website.

## Is it ever fine to pay to exhibit?

Sometimes. Paying for a defined exhibition service is reasonable when you know the full cost, the terms and what it’s worth to you.

Decide what you’re buying: a specific room, help with installation, a public event or another concrete service. Compare the price with other ways to get the same thing. Find out what happens if the show changes or is canceled.

Don’t pay for a vague promise of reputation. A venue in a well-known city doesn’t guarantee the right audience, press attention or future representation. Ask about opening hours, access, promotion and past shows. If sales matter to you, check the commission, how sales are handled and when you’re paid.

A disappointing show and a fraudulent one are different problems. You can turn down a poor-value offer without proving it’s a scam. Price is only one part of the check.

## How do I check an organizer before I pay?

Spend ten minutes checking the venue, the people, past shows, every fee and the terms, using sources other than the invitation itself.

Ten minutes won’t settle everything. If something important is still unclear, hold off on paying and keep looking.

| Check | Where to look | Good sign | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Venue and dates | The venue’s own program or contact page | The venue confirms the show and schedule | A vague “international gallery” with no named location |
| Organizer and jurors | Official site, staff pages, independent professional pages | Named people and a described selection process | An affiliation the institution won’t confirm |
| Past exhibitions | Archived programs and past artists’ own accounts | Consistent evidence the named shows happened | Images with no context, or claims past artists dispute |
| Every required fee | The call, application form and agreement | All costs disclosed before you apply | A new required package after “acceptance” |
| Sales commission | The written sales terms | A clear calculation and payment arrangement | Nobody can explain what you’ll actually receive |
| Rights and image use | Entry rules and the proposed contract | Permissions tied to this exhibition | Broad rights with no clear purpose or end date |
| Shipping and insurance | Delivery terms and the organizer’s written answer | Responsibility, return route and cover are clear | No return plan, or unclear damage liability |
| Other artists’ experience | Independent accounts and direct questions | Specific descriptions of what was delivered | Repeated, documented problems with the service |

Keep what you found separate from what you think. “The venue confirmed the booking” is a fact. “This seems worth my budget” is your decision. One good comment online shouldn’t outweigh an open question about payment or rights.

For a general check, use [how to check a call before you apply](/guides/verify-an-opportunity-before-applying). It covers current rules and where to apply. This guide is about identity, payment and misleading invitations.

## Does a Missa listing mean a call is safe?

Not on its own. A Missa listing links to the organizer’s page and marks what we couldn’t confirm, but it doesn’t guarantee you’re eligible or that the organizer will deliver.

You apply on the organizer’s page, not through Missa. The [methodology](/methodology) explains how calls are checked: the fee, dates and rules are each checked and shown on their own, and if two sources disagree, the listing says so. Read the organizer’s page before you apply. If the page doesn’t say, neither do we.

A listing isn’t a recommendation to pay. If one looks wrong, report it through the methodology page so it can be fixed. A current deadline and a clear application link are useful, but they can’t tell you whether a show is worth it for your work.

[No-fee calls](/discover/no-fee-calls) can cut what you spend on applications, but a free application isn’t a certificate of trust. Writers should also check the costs and terms in our [submission-fee guide](/guides/literary-magazine-submission-fees).

## What should I do if I’ve already paid?

Save the evidence and contact your bank, card issuer or payment service quickly if you think you paid a scammer.

1. **Save everything.** Keep the call, the agreement, the invoice, the receipt, the messages and where the payment went.
2. **Contact whoever handled the payment.** The US Federal Trade Commission’s [guide for people who’ve been scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed) sets out what to do for each payment method. What’s possible depends on how you paid and where you live, and getting the money back isn’t guaranteed.
3. **Ask the organizer in writing** about any service or refund you were promised and didn’t get. Keep it factual.
4. **Report impersonation.** If someone pretended to be a gallery or institution, tell that organization through its official contact details. You can also report it to your country’s fraud-reporting service. In the US, the FTC takes reports through [ReportFraud](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

Be wary of anyone who offers to get your money back for an upfront fee. The FTC’s [refund and recovery scam guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/refund-and-recovery-scams) says never to pay upfront for help getting a refund. Check any recovery service independently before you share information or pay again.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are all paid art competitions scams?

No. A fee can be part of a disclosed selection process. Check the organizer, the full terms and the value, not just the price.

### Does an exhibition in New York or London guarantee useful exposure?

No. Check the venue, the audience and the services. A city name doesn’t tell you who’ll come or what the organizer will deliver.

### Is an unnamed jury proof of a scam?

No, but it’s missing information. Check whether the selection process is explained, and ask the organizer before you pay.

### Can a call without a fee still be unsafe?

Yes. Identity, rights, requests for personal data and delivery terms still matter. Free entry doesn’t settle any of them.
